# PhysicsLab student perception test

Use this when a design decision depends on how the intended audience actually feels, not only on craft rules. It is a lightweight reality check, not a statistical claim.

## Participants

Prefer 5-8 Belarusian students aged 14-18. Include different grades and different confidence in physics. Do not recruit only olympiad students, designers, developers, or friends already familiar with the project.

Record age range, grade, whether the student is preparing for CT/CE, and how they currently feel about physics. Do not collect names or unnecessary personal data.

## First-impression test

Show one static screenshot for five seconds, then hide it. Do not explain the site's purpose, characters, intended mood, or teaching method beforehand.

Ask in this order:

1. What was this site about?
2. What did you notice first?
3. What do you remember seeing?
4. Who did the site seem made for?
5. Choose up to three words for how it felt.
6. Did anything feel childish, fake, confusing, or generated by AI? What exactly?

Use the same screenshot size and question order for every participant. Test the desktop and mobile compositions separately rather than showing both together.

## Return-after-school test

Show the live page and give a concrete situation without teaching the interface:

- "You did not understand this topic in class. Find where you would start."
- "You have a CT/CE practice session tonight. Find a task and begin."
- "You remember part of a formula but not the exact form. Find it."

Observe the first click, hesitation, backtracking, and what the student ignores. Ask afterward:

- What felt easiest to start?
- What made you hesitate?
- Did learning and exam preparation feel like different modes?
- What, if anything, would make you close the site?
- Would you open it again without a teacher telling you to? Why?

Do not rescue the participant during the first attempt unless they are fully stuck. A verbal explanation from the reviewer can hide a weak interface.

## Comparing visual directions

Compare two or three variants only when they solve the same problem and contain equivalent content. Randomize their order. Change one major variable at a time, such as composition, typography, or character crop.

Do not ask only "Which is prettier?" Ask separately:

- Which feels more pleasant?
- Which feels more coherent?
- Which feels more appropriate for your age?
- Which makes the purpose easier to understand?
- Which would you rather return to after school?
- What specific visible detail caused your choice?

Keep disagreements. Do not average unlike reasons into one beauty score.

## Interpreting results

- Repeated unaided observations are stronger than a single eloquent comment.
- Recall reveals hierarchy, not whether the remembered element was liked.
- Preference does not prove usability. First-click behavior and task completion remain separate evidence.
- One student's taste is a clue. A pattern across students is evidence.
- If students remember the cats but not physics, the characters are too dominant or too weakly connected to the subject.
- If students describe the site as a game, station, AI tutor, or site for young children, inspect imagery, copy, rounded shapes, motion, and reward language together rather than changing one token.

## Useful services

Maze and Lyssna can run consistent five-second and preference tests from screenshots. A plain timed presentation plus a form is sufficient for a small local study; the quality of participants and neutral questions matters more than the platform.
