# PhysicsLab aesthetic judgment

This review answers a human question: does the interface feel beautiful, coherent, authored, and believable for a Belarusian school student? It is intentionally separate from accessibility, performance, pixel diffs, and code quality.

Product identity and requirements come from `docs/current/`; this rubric does not override them. Mio is the current approved companion. An AI reviewer role-playing a student supplies a hypothesis, not participant evidence.

## Order of judgment

### 1. Look before explaining

Open a fresh screenshot without source code, DOM labels, test names, or performance scores. Record the first reaction before zooming in:

- what the eye notices in the first three seconds;
- where the eye goes next and where it gets lost;
- the one visual idea that remains in memory;
- whether the page feels authored, assembled from a template, or generated by an AI;
- the emotional temperature: inviting, curious, cozy, childish, corporate, theatrical, tiring, or indifferent.

Do not rewrite this first reaction after reading the code. Later evidence may explain it but must not erase it. Record the screenshot's source, route, state and commit when known. A historical screenshot or separate character sheet is not evidence of the current rendered application.

### 2. Test the composition at three distances

Inspect the same surface:

- as a thumbnail: the main mass, focal point, and negative space should still be legible;
- at normal viewing distance: hierarchy and reading order should feel effortless;
- close up: typography, image crop, edges, material, and micro-details should reward attention.

Also use a squint or blur reading. If every region has equal contrast and weight, the composition has no center. Use a grayscale reading when color may be hiding weak hierarchy.

Before explaining why the design works or fails, write a short structured description of the visible composition:

- the three largest visual masses and their approximate share of the viewport;
- the dominant axis or flow: horizontal, vertical, diagonal, circular, or scattered;
- the main groupings and the empty spaces separating them;
- overlaps, crops, and image-to-text relationships;
- which element owns the highest contrast and whether it deserves it.

This description must be factual rather than evaluative. It anchors the critique in visible relationships and prevents a generic design lecture from being projected onto the screenshot.

### 3. Judge the visual direction

Ask whether all decisions belong to one recognizable world:

- **Concept:** Is there a specific artistic premise beyond “illustrated educational website”?
- **Composition:** Do scale, overlap, alignment, cropping, and empty space create a deliberate scene?
- **Typography:** Does the type have a voice, and do prose, headings, controls, and mathematics coexist naturally?
- **Color and light:** Is the palette organized around a dominant atmosphere, or are accents distributed like UI tokens?
- **Material:** Do surfaces feel physically related, or like unrelated cards, gradients, glows, and borders?
- **Imagery:** Do characters and objects share an art direction, perspective, lighting, and level of finish?
- **Rhythm:** Does the page alternate quiet and dense moments, or repeat one component cadence?
- **Motion:** Does movement add anticipation, tactility, or meaning rather than constant decoration?
- **Detail:** Are there small, discoverable touches tied to physics and school life rather than generic “delight”?

### 4. Judge the product as a real place

Imagine a student actually returning after school, finding a difficult topic, making a mistake, and preparing for an exam.

- Does the site respect a teenager, or speak like a teacher, mascot app, startup, or children’s game?
- Does learning feel different from exam practice without a paragraph explaining the difference?
- Does Mio, and any explicitly approved supporting character, act with purpose rather than appear as a sticker placed in leftover space?
- Would the illustrations still make sense if printed in a Belarusian school workbook or shown to a local physics teacher?
- Are school objects, terminology, examples, clothes, rooms, and public transport culturally believable rather than vaguely American or fantastical?
- Can this art direction be maintained across dozens of topics, errors, diagrams, and states in real production?

### 5. Run the AI-slop credibility test

Call out the exact evidence, not a vague accusation. Common tells include:

- repeated equal cards and pill controls;
- decorative text explaining the intended vibe or teaching method;
- every section using the same heading, paragraph, and CTA rhythm;
- accents spread evenly instead of forming a focal hierarchy;
- generic cosmic gradients, glass panels, glow, or particles unrelated to the content;
- stock-looking characters with inconsistent anatomy, lighting, or visual language;
- illustrations used as filler rather than composing with the text and controls;
- over-rounded containers, wide soft shadows, excessive borders, and arbitrary badges;
- “cute” details that feel pasted on and do not help the student understand or act;
- an impressive hero followed by ordinary application screens from another product.

### 6. Use references without making a collage

Choose at most three references. Each reference must have one named job, for example:

- composition and use of negative space;
- character art direction and crop;
- editorial typography around mathematics;
- playful interaction without childishness.

State what must not be borrowed. Never combine whole visual languages from several references into one page.

Choose references by structural relevance, not merely by mood. A reference with the same image-to-text relationship, density, or interaction problem is more useful than a beautiful page that only shares the color palette.

### 7. Review through distinct craft lenses

Even when one reviewer performs the pass, keep the judgments separate before synthesizing them:

- composition and art direction;
- typography and mathematical notation;
- color, light, and material;
- illustration consistency, crop, and cultural credibility;
- adolescent product experience and learning/exam fit.

Do not force agreement. If a visual decision is memorable but hurts clarity, preserve both facts and state the tradeoff.

## Verdict format

Do not lead with a numeric beauty score; pseudo-precision makes subjective judgment less honest. Give:

1. **Gut reaction** — two or three direct sentences.
2. **Route of the eye** — the actual visual sequence and where it breaks.
3. **What feels authored** — the strongest specific choices worth protecting.
4. **What feels generated or fake** — exact elements and why.
5. **Composition and rhythm** — scale, spacing, density, repetition, and mobile changes.
6. **Typography and mathematics** — voice, readability, formula integration, indices, units, and hierarchy.
7. **Images and characters** — art direction, crop, placement, cultural credibility, and usefulness.
8. **Emotional fit** — how a Belarusian student might feel during learning and exam practice; label this as a hypothesis unless real participant evidence supports it.
9. **Keep / recompose / remove / regenerate** — a short decisive list tied to visible elements.
10. **Confidence and subjectivity** — distinguish clear craft failures from matters of taste.

End with the single change that would most improve the page’s perceived authorship. Technical failures may follow in a separate appendix but must not dilute the aesthetic verdict.

## Independent eyes

When the user authorizes sub-agents, keep the reviews unanchored:

- one reviewer acts as an art director and judges composition, type, imagery, and originality from screenshots only;
- one reviewer simulates a skeptical Belarusian secondary-school student to propose questions and likely problems, without presenting that simulation as user research;
- only after both finish may a third pass inspect code, tests, and deterministic detector findings.

Do not show one reviewer the conclusions of another before they submit their first reaction.
